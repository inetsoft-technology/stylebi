 Area fill &mdash; and filled line and radar with it 
 New visual property 
 The expensive one, and I had it ranked too low. AreaElement has no fill colour at all — it sets HINT_ALPHA 0.8 in an instance initialiser and paints the fill as the line&rsquo;s colour at 80% opacity. Giving the fill its own colour means a new property on the element and on AreaVO , not a lookup swap. It buys more than one chart type, though. LineElement has no fill of its own — a filled line and a radar are both AreaElement (radar is simply the zero-measure case, isRadar() returns getVarCount() == 0 ), so one property covers area, filled line and filled radar together. Worth noting LineElement carries a second opacity-for-colour case in fillLineAlpha 0.3 , the wash over null-gap segments — same substitution, different symptom. 
 Every case above is grounded in code that exists — AreaElement , TargetForm bands, ConfidenceIntervalStrategy , BrushingColor . None is a hypothetical feature, which also means none can be deferred on the grounds that nobody uses it. Phase 0 alone is worth shipping: with the accessor in place, phase 1 is a two-line wiring change.

 3f 
 Multi-level charts 
 icicle and sunburst — one rule, two geometries 
 Colour comes from a single bound dimension, so what goes wrong depends on which one an author picks. Bind the leaf dimension and the palette restarts at every level, leaving a child with no visual link to its parent. Bind the root dimension and the whole branch turns one flat colour, so nothing distinguishes a ring from the ring outside it. Neither encodes depth, and neither is fixed by adding colours — what is missing is a rule for what colour means at each level.

 With the leaf dimension bound, every region&rsquo;s children begin at slot 1 — so the first child of North America and the first child of LATAM are the same blue. Colour repeats without meaning anything and the eye cannot follow a branch outward.

 A branch is one hue from root to leaf, so the eye follows it outward without a legend. Depth reads as recession — the further from the root, the closer to the canvas. Both bindings below collapse into the same rule; treemap and circle packing turn out not to need it.

 Bind colour to the root dimension and a branch is already one flat hue all the way out — inheritance is not the thing that is missing. What is missing is everything inside the branch: three rings of one colour say nothing about which ring is which, and two adjacent children are told apart only by the white stroke between them. The ladder spends that flat colour on depth and sibling instead of leaving it unused. 
 Treemap and circle packing are out of scope

 Treemap and circle packing are containment types — children are drawn inside their parent — so only leaves are ever filled with a data colour. A parent gets a border whose width steps with depth ( min(level+1, 5) ) and, optionally, a fixed per-level background that is a theme colour rather than a series colour. Labels follow the same rule: hasTextLabel returns isLeaf() for treemap. Depth there is carried by nested border weight, not hue — one colour level, as you thought . Sunburst and icicle are not containment types, so every node is filled and the ladder applies. 
 One thing still worth taking from it. Treemap leaves are coloured by sequential leaf index, so two leaves under different parents can land on the same colour and nothing ties a leaf to its branch. Inheriting the ancestor&rsquo;s hue and spreading siblings within one band — the same rule collapsed to a single level — would fix that without touching the containment rendering. Not built here; flagging it rather than assuming it.

The white stroke between slices keeps them from literally merging, so this is the weakest of the three signals — but without it a legend entry and its neighbour are the same swatch, and a reader comparing two siblings has only position to go on.  When levels are Use Why 
 One dimension, recursively 
 Org &rarr; sub-org, category &rarr; sub-category. Inheritance is the point: the child is part of the parent, so it should look like it. 
 Two different dimensions 
 Country &rarr; Industry. Industry is comparable across countries, so it needs its own identity rather than a shade of its parent. Level 1 takes Default , level 2 takes Soft — no new palette, and the chroma gap means a level-2 colour can never be mistaken for a level-1 one. 
 Past the limit 
 Deeper or wider than the ladder supports, or below roughly 8px: drop to alternating neutrals and let geometry and the tooltip carry it. A three-pixel slice conveys nothing by hue no matter which colour is in it.

The larger question is upstream — TreemapGeometry already carries getLevel() and getChildRows() , but nothing passes depth, breadth or sibling index into colour resolution, so TreemapVisualModel.getColor falls through to the ordinary categorical lookup for every node.

 not categorical

 Property of the loader What it means Consequence for this redesign 
 Length = highest index Color[] colors = new Color[max] — the palette is as long as its largest declared index, not as long as its count of rules. Our sets declare 1&ndash;8 and are eight long. A stray [index=40] would produce a forty-slot palette with thirty-one nulls. 
 Indexes are 1-based, gaps are null Anything below 1 is skipped, and a missing index leaves a null in the array rather than collapsing. A deployment that overrides 1, 2 and 5 gets nulls at 3 and 4. Worth a validation warning; the loader itself does not complain. 
 Per organization paletteMap is keyed by OrganizationManager.getCurrentOrgID() , and reports resolve through a separate cssLocation . Palette sets are per-tenant, not global. Two orgs on one install can ship different palettes — which is the real answer to &ldquo;our customer needs their own colours.&rdquo; 
 Overriding a generated slot Declaring [index=9] makes max nine, so the slot becomes real rather than generated. This resolves the question I left open in 3e : generated colours are themeable by declaring them, and generation simply resumes past whatever the deployment defined. No bound to change. 
 The one gap is companions. The selector carries name and index and resolves to a single colour, so there is nowhere in this schema for a slot's soft variant. Two options: teach CSSDictionary a third attribute ( ChartPalette[name=Default][index=3][variant=soft] ), which is the honest model but touches the parser; or use a parallel palette name — Default-soft , loaded by the existing code with no change at all. The second costs nothing and is how I would ship it; 3j works it through, including why the authored palette should be the override rather than the mechanism.

 The one thing to get right in the picker. getPaletteNames() returns every name in the CSS, so Default-soft would appear in Select Palette as a choosable palette. Better to filter in getPaletteNames() itself and expose companions only through a getCompanionPalette(name) accessor, so the reserved suffix is enforced in one place rather than assumed by every caller.

 C 
 Ink and electric 
 adopted 
 a dark anchor, high chroma, wide lightness spread 
 The honest critique of A is that its eight hues are stock — recognisable from any modern component library, safe rather than authored. C makes two moves A would not. One slot is a near-black ink rather than a colour, giving a chart a graphic anchor — and at C 0.09 it is deliberately the least saturated swatch in either palette, because an anchor that competes is not an anchor. The rest run hotter: mean chroma 0.20 across slots 2–8 against A's 0.18, with violet at 0.25 and magenta at 0.24 sitting near the sRGB boundary. But the real separation is lightness — C spans L 0.27 (ink) to 0.81 (amber), a range of 0.54 , against A's 0.53–0.73, a range of 0.21. Two and a half times the vertical room.

 Four series drawn in order: indigo, burnt orange, teal, raspberry. Even, competent, and it could be any BI product shipped since 2019. 
 C — ink and electric 
 adopted 
 Same four slots: azure, coral, ink, teal. The ink anchors the chart so the colours read as accents against it rather than as four equal claims — which is the whole difference in feel. 
 What C costs, plainly. Ink is nearly the text colour, so a chart and its labels start sharing a value — fine on the warm canvas, risky on white cards. High chroma is fatiguing across a twelve-widget dashboard in a way A is not; that is partly what Soft exists for, but it does push more work onto the author's palette choice. And the lightness spread that makes C graphic is the same property that makes its lightest members thin on a projector — Contrast stays the answer there. My read: C is right if StyleBI wants charts that look like its charts; A is right if the priority is never being the reason someone dislikes a dashboard.

 What it costs 
 Eight hues now share 290° instead of 360°, so adjacent members sit ~36° apart rather than ~45°. The lightness ladder carries the difference — L spans 0.38 to 0.84 , a range of 0.46 against A's 0.21 — which is why this reads as separated despite the narrower arc. 
 No gold, no olive, no brown. Charts lose a warm mid-tone they have always had, and some customers will read that as the palette missing something. The answer is that it is not missing — it is reserved. 
 Also fixes C's ink. The coordination doc names shell neutral surfaces and border greys as colours that must not become visualization colours; C's ink at L 0.27 sat in text-strong 's territory at L 0.24. D's darkest member is plum at L 0.38 — dark enough to anchor, clear of the text ramp.
